# Purpose and Domains

This document answers the question the other three don't: what is Life OS trying to do, for whom, and what does it mean for the app to be working? `ARCHITECTURE.md` explains how the code is arranged; `RECENT-CHANGES.md` explains what changed and why recently; the feature guide explains what every button does. None of them tell you what a *correct* change looks like when the code doesn't spell it out — that's this document's job.

## What Life OS is

Life OS is a single-user personal operating system built for one person, Ayman. It is not a product in any commercial sense — there is no signup flow for strangers, no growth loop, no plan tiers, and per the founding design spec it is explicitly single-user by decision, not by omission: "no multi-tenant auth, no accounts beyond the owner," with multi-user support and social/sharing features named directly as non-goals from the first spec onward.

This matters for how you should reason about changes here. "Would this scale to more users?" and "what if someone else logs in?" are not just unanswered questions in this codebase — they are the *wrong* questions. A feature that would be a liability at scale (data that never expires, a page that hand-computes something on every load instead of caching it, a form with only one plausible answer pre-filled) is often simply fine here, because there is exactly one account that will ever see it, and it's usually seen by that one person the same day it changes.

Row-level security is still enabled across the schema — you'll find it in most migrations. Don't read that as evidence of hidden multi-tenancy or a pivot in progress: it's defense in depth on a database with one real tenant, not infrastructure being kept warm for a second one. If you find yourself designing around "what happens when user B exists," you've drifted from the actual problem this app solves.

The founding spec also describes the intended shape directly: a system that closes a loop the previous state of Ayman's life didn't close — plans made and then never checked, goals set and then never consulted, a day that ends without ever being looked back on. Much of what looks unusual about this codebase (the nightly Review, the forced-rewrite logic on a distraction plan, Home's insistence on showing exactly one next action) exists because an internal power-architecture review of the app's own first several weeks concluded, in its own words, that "Life OS records well and decides poorly" — every screen answered *what happened*, almost nothing answered *what should I do now*. Everything built since has been, in large part, a correction of that.

## The five domains

Life OS divides Ayman's life into five areas, each with its own screen, and treats them as genuinely separate concerns rather than five views onto one generic "tasks" table.

**Deen** governs the five daily prayers, Qur'an reading, a habit-builder for religious practice, and a private daily reflection log. Its stated purpose is not compliance-checking — the feature guide is explicit that "everything on this screen exists to answer one question honestly: 'how am I actually doing,' not just 'did I check a box today.'" That framing shows up structurally: a missed prayer isn't hidden, a Qur'an streak has no freeze mechanic, and the private reflection log exists specifically so something sensitive doesn't have to compete for space with a public-feeling tracker. Success here is measured in honesty and consistency, not completion — the app would rather show "0/5, not tracked yet" than a discouraging false zero, and would rather break a streak outright than let a freeze mechanic quietly launder a missed day.

**Work** governs Ayman's job — a single active priority ("Target") at a time, a short queue of what's next, and a four-stage pipeline of tasks underneath it. The domain's whole design rests on one idea, stated in the feature guide itself: you can only truly focus on one thing at once, so the screen is built to make that one thing physically larger and harder to lose track of than everything on deck behind it. Success is forward motion on the one thing that currently matters, not breadth — the interface actively resists showing you an undifferentiated list of everything you could be doing.

**Business/Lock-In** governs deep-work and deep-study sessions and a daily three-item "kill list." The founding synthesis for this domain states its spine directly: "every day you can only truly protect three priorities" — and elsewhere states, as a design commitment rather than an incidental choice, that accumulated focus hours must never be "presented as an achievement... as *context for output*, never as a score," because a metric that becomes a score invites gaming it. This is the one domain where the record-keeping is explicitly in service of self-honesty about effort, not motivation through progress bars.

**School** governs classes, assignments, tests, and syllabi. Its job is comparatively mechanical next to the other four — due dates, overdue counts, and one place per class to find the syllabus and schedule — but the underlying intent is the same one that runs through Home: don't make Ayman remember where something lives or whether it's coming due, surface it.

**Fitness** governs training plans, a daily log, and a 28-day measurement cycle. The feature guide states its organizing idea plainly: "the app should never let you fool yourself about whether you trained today," and should show honest numbers — sets, weight, waist, rep maxes — "rather than a vague sense of progress." It deliberately supports two very different training styles (quick daily rep targets and structured multi-set sessions) because Ayman genuinely uses both, which is itself a small instance of a larger pattern: this app models the actual person, not a template of a person.

## The cross-cutting systems, and the failure mode each one exists to close

These aren't domains — they cut across all five, and each one exists because something specific used to fail without it.

**Home** is the single daily entry point specifically because checking five separate screens every time the app opens didn't happen — the day's actual priorities got missed by omission, not by decision. An internal design review names Home's job as exactly three things in priority order — what do I do right now, where am I in the day, how do I get to detail if I need it — and states that anything on the screen that isn't one of those three is a candidate for removal. That's a stronger claim than "Home is a dashboard": Home is explicitly not supposed to be one. Metrics and history belong in Insights; Home's job is the next action.

**The nightly Review** exists because a distraction noticed in the moment is cheap to log and easy to never think about again — the app's own retrospective on itself notes plainly that before this existed, "there is no moment where the day gets closed." The review forces every recurring trigger from that day into an actual plan, and — this is the sharper piece of it — a plan that has failed three or more times with zero successes gets force-rewritten rather than allowed to be silently re-skipped again, because a plan you keep failing and keep excusing is worse than no plan.

**Distractions capture** is the front half of the same system: a one-tap, no-navigation way to note "this just happened" without breaking whatever Ayman is doing, including inside a locked-in focus session. It exists because friction at the moment of capture is what makes things simply not get written down — one of the app's own internal reviews frames "capture friction" as upstream of everything else that depends on the data being real.

**Check-ins** exist so that the app's picture of how time was actually spent doesn't rely on Ayman reconstructing it from memory at the end of the day — periodic, low-friction prompts sample reality as it happens (deliberately modeled, per the founding spec, on periodic-sampling tools rather than continuous tracking) so that Signal:Noise and Insights reflect what actually happened rather than a flattering guess.

**Notifications** exist to be the thing that reaches out, rather than requiring Ayman to remember to open the app at the right moment — the review window, a check-in prompt, something waiting for action, all surfaced without him having to go looking.

**Calendar and Insights** are the two backward/forward-looking views, and they're kept deliberately separate from Home for the same reason Home excludes metrics: Insights is where the numbers add up and patterns surface; Calendar is where the whole week's shape — classes, shifts, workouts, deadlines — is visible at once. Both exist because neither kind of looking-around belongs on the one screen whose entire job is telling Ayman what to do in this exact moment.

## Design principles

A few principles are strongly and repeatedly evidenced in the app's own design history, not just inferred from patterns in the code:

- **Mobile-first, specifically at 390px**, is a stated design mandate, not an incidental bug source — an internal refactor spec requires "every screen must be designed at 390px, not merely not-broken," and acceptance criteria for at least one Home feature explicitly gate on fitting at 390px without scrolling. 390px is Ayman's actual phone.
- **Speed of capture over completeness.** Multiple specs state this directly: "fast, frictionless logging," single-tap and single-select interactions "to keep this friction-free," and a kill-list entry flow explicitly redesigned to lower how much effort it takes to write something down, not to make the form more thorough.
- **The app should tell Ayman what to do next, not present a dashboard.** This is close to the most explicit and most repeated principle in the app's own design record — stated as Home's entire mandate, and stated as the core diagnosis (the app "records well and decides poorly") that motivated a large share of the work that followed it.
- **Accountability over gamification, specifically and by name.** Points, badges, and levels are explicitly rejected for Deen as "the wrong register for a system that tracks religious practice," on the grounds that a number that becomes a score corrupts the honesty of the data underneath it — and the same rule, never present an input metric as an achievement, is reinvented independently for Business focus-hours ("as *context for output*, never as a score"). Two domains arriving at the same rule without cross-referencing each other is what makes this a principle rather than a preference; treat it as settled enough to design against. The one place it's still explicitly open is a habit-builder proposal that frames "no badges" as its author's judgment call pending Ayman's sign-off, not a closed decision.

One candidate principle worth naming because it did *not* hold up: **"nothing should require a decision late at night" is not a real design rule here.** There's no evidence for it anywhere in the specs, and the nightly Review actively cuts against it — it's deliberately scheduled in the evening and does ask Ayman to make real decisions then (tomorrow's priorities, plan rewrites). If a future agent assumes late-evening interactions should be avoided or simplified for this reason, that assumption isn't supported by anything in this app's actual design.

## Objectives and how the app measures them

The app runs on several different cadences at once, deliberately, rather than one universal reset clock:

- **Daily**: the Business kill list resets every calendar day and does not carry unfinished items forward — the app forces a fresh, deliberate re-prioritization each morning rather than letting yesterday's leftovers silently become today's default.
- **Weekly, Sunday–Saturday**: headline weekly goals for Deen and Business (the only two domains with a weekly-goal slot on Home), the Business Signal:Noise ratio, and Fitness's weekly workout count all reset on this boundary.
- **Rolling 30-day windows, not hard resets**: Deen's reflection tracking and Qur'an-adjacent habit tracking are explicitly built as "last 30 days" rolling rates rather than streaks, a deliberate choice to avoid the all-or-nothing fragility of a hard reset.
- **Elapsed-time stages, not streaks**: the Habit Builder's Active Build → Stabilized → Locked progression is computed purely from days elapsed since a habit's committed start date, and a streak explicitly does not gate that promotion — consistency over time outranks an unbroken run.
- **28-day training cycles**: Fitness's cycle clock is anchored to the date the first plan was ever activated and keeps counting in the background even through periods with no active plan, so the measurement period is stable regardless of whether Ayman is currently using the feature.

The throughline is that the app doesn't treat "current streak" as the universal scoreboard — several domains were deliberately built to avoid exactly that fragility, in favor of measures that tolerate a bad day without erasing a good month.

## What this app is deliberately not

The app's own design history names several directions it considered and rejected, which is worth knowing precisely because a plausible-sounding feature request can point straight at one of them: gamification (points, badges, levels — rejected by name); a general LLM chat surface ("the references have one; it would be decoration here"); full offline-first sync (rejected as enormous complexity for a single user on two devices with good connectivity); calendar import from external services (real value, but its own integration project, not worth the auth/sync surface for what it would add); any form of multi-user or social/sharing feature; a global search field (rejected specifically because a single-user, nine-screen app doesn't have enough surface area to search); widening the Business kill list past three items (called out as the one place the product already, deliberately, says no); exercise-level set/rep/weight tracking as the default fitness model (fitness stays at the habit/schedule level by design, with structured workouts as an addition, not the base case); and automated fiqh logic for prayer-shortening rules, left to manual judgment because that's the kind of thing that's easy to get subtly and invisibly wrong by automating.

## Where the source of truth lives

Design intent, including the reasoning above, lives in `docs/superpowers/specs/` — read chronologically, it's effectively a decision log, not just a features list, and several files there explicitly revisit and revise earlier ones. `PROJECT_STATUS.md` is the historical build log — what shipped and when, not why. `AGENTS.md` holds the hard operational rules (timezone handling, the RSC prop-serialization trap, shared-worktree git discipline, the e2e-suite trust rules) that have each been paid for in a real production incident. The feature guide (`Life OS — Complete Feature Guide.docx`) is the behavioral ground truth for what the app currently does, verified against the running app screen by screen. When any of these appear to disagree, the specs tell you what was intended, `PROJECT_STATUS.md` and the feature guide tell you what actually exists, and the gap between them — where one exists — is usually more informative than either document alone.
